Does the labyrinth in Borges represent spatial geometry or temporal branching?

MA
Marcus Aureliano Patron #402 • Verified Custodian
Archive of Speculative Thought • Senior Collegiate Fellow
verified Custodial Inscription Seal #A-8921
format_quote

«In all fictional works, each time a man is confronted with several alternatives, he chooses one and eliminates the others; in the fiction of Ts'ui Pên, he chooses — simultaneously — all of them. He creates, in this way, diverse futures, diverse times which themselves also proliferate and fork.»

Ficciones • "The Garden of Forking Paths", p. 114 Examine Folio Marginalia north_east

When Ts'ui Pên retired from his governorship to draft a boundless labyrinth and author a manuscript that appeared incomprehensible, his contemporaries presumed these were two disparate architectural undertakings. It is Stephen Albert who delivers the seminal revelation: the labyrinth and the book are an identical artifact. Yet, when contemporary readers re-examine Borges’ geometric depictions—the infinite concentric corridors, the solitary Chinese pavilion, the garden paths that continually bifurcate—we are confronted by an unresolved ontological paradox.

Is this maze a tangible, topological labyrinth that collapses Euclidean distances through sheer recursive complexity, or is geometry merely a sensory metaphor Borges employs to articulate non-linear duration? If Ts’ui Pên does not eliminate alternatives but simultaneously inhabits their permutations, the maze cannot occupy three-dimensional space without invoking a quantum superposition of realities. Space in Borges is invariably static, dusty, and bounded by plaster walls, while time is the fluid, corrosive substance that shatters character identity.

I propose that the garden is not a physical enclosure situated within the suburbs of Hai Feng, but an asynchronous temporal tapestry. Every junction we mistake for an intersection of gravel is actually an epistemic crisis: an instant where two observers converse as allies in one branch and execute one another in another. Does the text support this complete dematerialization of space, or did Borges intentionally construct the labyrinth as an impossible synthesis where space and time become indistinguishable?

Folio Ref: §IV.12

Marcus raises the quintessential inquiry. However, I would caution against divesting the labyrinth of its physical geometry entirely. Borges had an intense preoccupation with Henri Bergson's concept of durée (duration) versus spatialized time.

In Chapter 4 of the manuscript drafts, Ts'ui Pên writes of a maze in which "all men would lose their way." That loss of bearing is profoundly tactile—it requires the physical fatigue of turning left at every crossroads. If the labyrinth were purely metaphysical time, the dramatic tension of Captain Richard Madden hunting Yu Tsun through the English countryside would lose its horrifying rhythmic pulse. The labyrinth functions precisely because the physical landscape mirrors the ontological branching.

Yet Clara, isn't that physical hunting sequence intentionally subverted at the pavilion? When Stephen Albert describes the Chinese labyrinth, he reminds us that it is not round or square, but coextensive with the universe itself. If a maze is infinite and coextensive with all existence, "spatial turning" ceases to have measurable mathematical coordinates. It becomes an infinite set of probabilities.
